OptoSAR funding, a packaging-heavy chip mission, and Hyderabad's GaN bet

October 5, 2026  |  7 min read

Three things from the past week in Indian space and semiconductors, written down with the facts and figures that actually back them, and a short note on each from where I sit as an RTL/RF design engineer.

1. GalaxEye's OptoSAR gets funded to grow up

Bengaluru — Oct 2 & Oct 4, 2026

  • GalaxEye secured $6.5M in government support from India's Technology Development Board (TDB), part of a $25.5M total project, to take its OptoSAR satellite tech from Technology Readiness Level 6 to TRL 9.
  • OptoSAR fuses Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) with electro-optical (EO) imaging on one satellite, targeting sub-0.5 m resolution across a wider range of weather and lighting conditions than either sensor alone.
  • Separately reported this week: a US patent for OptoSAR granted September 2026, a plan for a 30-satellite constellation over five years, the August 2026 acquisition of StarOps for manufacturing, and ₹638.4 million in TDB venture debt from September 2026.
  • Also on record: GalaxEye's first satellite, Mission Drishti (launched May 2026), lost communication in July 2026 after a solar storm, after reportedly completing around 85% of its planned objectives.

My take — Every writeup of this repeats "radar plus optical equals all-weather imaging" like that's the hard part. It isn't. SAR wants a stable, off-nadir incidence angle; EO wants near-nadir. Reconciling both on one bus and one attitude profile, then co-registering two very different data products, is a geometry and signal-processing problem before it's ever a sales pitch. TRL 6 to TRL 9 is where that gets paid for — vibration, thermal, radiation qualification, none of which makes it into a press release. The more interesting line in this story isn't the patent, it's that Mission Drishti lost its comms link to a solar storm after doing most of its job. "All-weather" imaging getting taken out by actual weather in space is worth sitting with.

2. India has 12 approved chip units. Nine of them don't design chips.

New Delhi — Oct 2, 2026

  • Rajeev Chandrasekhar — former Union IT minister, credited with helping build the India Semiconductor Mission — said the strategy needs to move beyond chip packaging.
  • The numbers: 12 approved manufacturing units, ₹1.64 lakh crore in committed investment, 5 facilities already operational. Nine of the 12 are ATMP/OSAT (assembly, test, mark, pack) — what he called the lower-margin end of the value chain, facing rising competition.
  • His proposed fix leans on ISM 2.0's additional ₹1.275 lakh crore: a National Semiconductor Research Institute, a ₹1,000-crore Chip Design Commercialisation Fund, and sovereign AI-inference-chip programs with government offtake guarantees.
  • He points to 1.25 lakh chip-design engineers and the open-source RISC-V DIR-V program as underused assets.

My take — I'm an RTL/FPGA engineer, which puts me adjacent to, not inside, the number he's citing. There's a real gap between "can write synthesizable Verilog for an FPGA bitstream" and "chip-design engineer" in the sense this policy wants: DFT, static timing closure on a specific process node, floorplanning, tape-out. FPGA shops are quietly where a decent chunk of that muscle gets built in India right now, almost by accident. 1.25 lakh is a headcount number, not a capability number, and a country can announce as many fabs as it wants — it can't announce its way to a design ecosystem. Worth watching whether the next ₹1.275 lakh crore buys that muscle or just more buildings.

3. A Hyderabad startup signs up to build India's own GaN

Hyderabad — Oct 2-3, 2026

  • SpintronicsAI Semiconductors (Hyderabad) signed an MoU with CSIR-CEERI (Pilani) to develop gallium nitride (GaN) technology and assess the feasibility of indigenous GaN fabrication — reportedly the first Indian company to partner with CEERI on this specifically.
  • Stated applications: power electronics, RF/communications, telecom infrastructure, defence, aerospace, satellite systems, electric mobility.
  • India's GaN market is pegged at roughly $224M in 2026, growing to about $1.88B by 2033, per SpintronicsAI's own figures.
  • Context: in mid-September 2026, DRDO's Solid State Physics Laboratory reported demonstrating indigenous GaN-on-SiC MMIC tech on 4-inch SiC wafers — individual die up to 30 W, substrate-based HEMTs to 150 W, X-band MMICs at 40 W. This MoU is one more front in a multi-institution push, not an isolated deal.

My take — I've designed RF clocking chains and done RFSoC DAC/ADC bring-up, so I'll say plainly why GaN-on-SiC is worth the fuss: higher breakdown field and power density than GaAs, far better thermal conductivity than GaN-on-silicon — which is exactly why it's the substrate of choice for a compact, high-power RF PA in a satellite transponder or radar transmitter. None of that is in question. What's in question is the distance between "we signed an MoU to explore fabrication feasibility" and a qualified, yield-stable part you'd actually spec into an RF chain — wafer supply at volume, packaging, reliability qualification. This is happening in my own city, which is a nice hook and not the point. The point is checking back on this in a year for a parts list, not a press release.

Busy week on the India space/semiconductor-policy front, concentrated rather than spread evenly. Nothing new surfaced this week in global RFSoC/EDA silicon or RF/DSP open-source releases worth including here.
